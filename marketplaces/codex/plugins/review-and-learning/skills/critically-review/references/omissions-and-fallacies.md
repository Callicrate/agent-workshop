# Omissions, Manipulation Patterns, and Informal Fallacies

This file helps catch what the document hides, downplays, or reasons through badly.

## Material omissions checklist

Ask whether the artifact omits any of the following:

- baseline or counterfactual
- denominator or base rate
- confidence interval, uncertainty, or variance
- alternative explanations
- failure modes or edge cases
- costs, switching costs, or operational burden
- risks to safety, privacy, security, or compliance
- tradeoffs across stakeholder groups
- historical context or previous failed attempts
- implementation dependencies, maintenance, or staffing requirements
- time horizon and timing assumptions
- distribution shift or non-representative sample concerns
- competitor or substitute options
- what evidence would falsify the recommendation

A statement can be literally true and still materially misleading if these omissions matter to the decision.

## Common argumentative defects

### Strawman

The artifact attacks a weakened or distorted version of a competing position.

To justify this label, identify:

- the rival position being portrayed
- how the portrayal is weaker or narrower than the real one
- why knocking it down does not answer the stronger version

### Cherry-picking

The artifact selects favorable examples, windows, benchmarks, or case studies while ignoring contrary evidence.

Typical forms:

- choosing only favorable time windows
- comparing against weak baselines
- highlighting one study while ignoring the rest of the literature
- spotlighting a success case while hiding base rates or failures

### False dichotomy

The artifact presents two options as exhaustive when reasonable alternatives exist.

### Equivocation

A key term shifts meaning mid-argument: accuracy, safety, reasoning, automation, efficiency, adoption, or productivity often hide this move.

### Causal overreach

The artifact infers causation from correlation, anecdote, before-after contrast, or uncontrolled comparison.

### Survivorship bias

The analysis includes only visible winners, retained users, shipped projects, or successful pilots while ignoring the failures.

### Motte-and-bailey

A bold claim is used for persuasion, but when challenged the author retreats to a narrower, easier-to-defend claim.

### Moving the goalposts

Evaluation criteria change once the original claim is weakened.

### Confusing necessary and sufficient conditions

The document treats one helpful factor as if it guarantees the outcome.

### Appeal to authority

The artifact leans on prestige, title, or brand instead of the actual reasoning.

### Hasty generalization

A small pilot, anecdote, or narrow sample is used to support broad general conclusions.

### Nirvana fallacy

Realistic alternatives are rejected because they are imperfect, while the document's preferred option is treated as if idealized assumptions will hold.

## Framing and spin patterns

Look for these rhetorical moves:

- passive voice hiding ownership of a bad assumption
- adjectives substituting for evidence: robust, transformative, inevitable, safe, objective
- unsupported novelty claims: first, unique, category-defining
- metrics that sound impressive but are unmoored from outcomes
- selective use of percentages without absolute values
- presenting pilot success as production readiness
- burying caveats in footnotes or appendices while selling the headline result

## Reviewer caution

Not every weak argument deserves a formal fallacy label. Use labels when they clarify the reasoning error, not when they merely intensify the tone.
